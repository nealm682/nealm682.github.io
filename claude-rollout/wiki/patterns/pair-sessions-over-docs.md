# Onboarding is pairing, not documentation

Asked how to onboard people who had never touched the tool, three practitioners independently gave
the same answer and none of them said "write a guide."

## Evidence

`thlandgraf`, with the reason attached — which is the useful part:

> "For onboarding, pair sessions beat docs. Have people watch someone experienced for 20 minutes
> then swap. The hardest thing for new users is calibrating how much context to give and that's
> learned by watching, not reading."

`_Bo_Knows` reaches for a sports/Twitch analogy:

> "You can play around with the game and eventually learn how get success, or you can turn on Twitch
> or TV and see how the best people in the world use it. It gives you an idea of what's possible."

`paulcaplan`, in full: *"The most helpful thing is pairing sessions."*

`HaagNDaazer` on hearing it: *"I like the idea of buddy vibing, I'm definitely stealing that!"*

## Implications for my design

The stated reason matters more than the recommendation. **The hard skill is calibrating how much
context to give**, and that is tacit — which means it is subject to exactly the same expert blind
spot the whole knowledge layer exists to work around. You cannot write it down usefully, so you
demonstrate it.

Concretely: my pilot cohort design already mixes experience levels, but I have been treating that as
politics (champions, adoption signal). This says it is also the *training mechanism* — the
experienced pilot members are how everyone after them learns. That argues for running the rollout in
waves seeded by pilot members rather than as a single training session.

It also lowers the value of the beautifully-produced onboarding explainer I would instinctively
build. Twenty minutes of shoulder-surfing beats it.
